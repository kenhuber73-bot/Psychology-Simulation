What "learning rate" means here: after every round, a model was fit to your choices — the same kind of model (a Rescorla–Wagner rule) researchers use to study how people learn from outcomes. Your α tells us how much you updated your belief about which machine was better after each single result. A high α means you swung your beliefs quickly based on recent outcomes. A low α means you leaned more on your longer-run impression and were slower to shift.

Why this matters: this task is a simplified version of a real study (Browning et al., 2015, Nature Neuroscience). In the original, participants learned which of two shapes predicted a mild shock, and researchers found that people with higher anxiety tended to have learning rates that changed less between predictable and unpredictable environments — they were slower to notice when the "rules" had shifted. One idea researchers have proposed is that this isn't about learning fear more strongly, but about how well the brain tracks how uncertain the world currently is.

Your single α from one short game isn't a diagnosis of anything — it's just one number, from one sitting, with a lot of noise in it. Real research studies compare group averages across many people and conditions before drawing conclusions.
