The Two-Armed Bandit

You're a small-time gambler on the casino floor with $500 in chips. Two slot machines sit in front of you. One pays out more often than the other — but you don't know which, and you won't be told.

Pick a machine, place a bet, and pull the lever. Try to walk away with more than you started with. Nothing here is announced to you in advance — you'll have to learn as you go.

Before you play, you'll fill out a short, real anxiety questionnaire. After you play, you'll write a short reflection. At the end, you'll see your results side by side with your questionnaire score.

Anxiety and learning rate, side by side: this task is a simplified version of a real study (Browning et al., 2015, Nature Neuroscience). In the original, participants learned which of two shapes predicted a mild shock, and researchers found that people with higher anxiety tended to have learning rates that changed less between predictable and unpredictable environments — they were slower to notice when the "rules" had shifted. One idea researchers have proposed is that this isn't about learning fear more strongly, but about how well the brain tracks how uncertain the world currently is.

Your own pair of numbers above isn't a diagnosis, and it isn't evidence of anything by itself — it's two numbers, from one sitting, with a lot of noise in both. Real research studies compare group averages across dozens or hundreds of people before drawing conclusions like the one above.
