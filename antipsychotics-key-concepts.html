Read: Typical vs. Atypical

Typical (1st-generation) antipsychotics
Drugs like chlorpromazine and haloperidol work mainly by blocking D2 dopamine receptors. Blocking D2 reduces dopamine signaling, which can reduce positive symptoms of schizophrenia (hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking) by decreasing excessive dopamine activity in pathways associated with them. Because typical antipsychotics block D2 receptors broadly — including in movement-related pathways — they carry a higher risk of extrapyramidal (movement-related) side effects.
Atypical (2nd-generation) antipsychotics
Drugs like clozapine, risperidone, and olanzapine still block D2 receptors, reducing dopamine signaling the same way typical antipsychotics do. They also block serotonin 5-HT2A receptors — this doesn't "increase serotonin," it blocks certain serotonin receptors, and that blockade alters dopamine regulation in other brain regions. This second mechanism is associated with a lower risk of movement-related side effects compared with many typical antipsychotics, while remaining effective against positive symptoms.
A note on the dopamine hypothesis
The dopamine hypothesis links excessive dopamine activity (especially in the mesolimbic pathway) to positive symptoms of schizophrenia. This is a useful model, not the full story — modern research suggests schizophrenia involves a more complex interaction across multiple neurotransmitter systems and brain circuits.
