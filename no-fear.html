The Woman With No Fear

A case study in interpretation, told in five parts

A retelling of the real, published case of S.M. (also called SM-046), the most extensively studied patient in the neuroscience of fear. All events described are drawn from decades of published research on her case. Read each part in order, answer the questions, and discuss with your group when prompted before moving on.
PART ONE

The Patient Known as S.M.

S.M. is an American woman first described in the scientific literature in 1994. As a young child, she was diagnosed with an extremely rare genetic condition called Urbach-Wiethe disease. In most patients, the disease mainly affects the skin and vocal cords — but in S.M., it did something else: it slowly calcified and destroyed a small, almond-shaped structure deep in each side of her brain, called the amygdala. By late childhood, both her left and right amygdalae were completely gone.

Everything else about her brain — memory, language, reasoning, movement — developed normally. She grew up, went to school, made friends, had a family. There was only one thing consistently, strikingly different about her, something researchers would spend the next three decades documenting in extraordinary detail.

The media eventually gave her a nickname:

"The Woman With No Fear."

PART TWO

Testing the Limits

Researchers didn't just ask S.M. whether she felt afraid — they tried to actually scare her, using some of the most reliable fear-inducing stimuli known to psychology:

  • She was taken to an exotic pet store and asked to handle live snakes and spiders, including large tarantulas — animals that trigger near-universal disgust and fear responses in humans, even those who've never been bitten.
  • She was walked through Waverly Hills Sanatorium, a real abandoned tuberculosis hospital used as a haunted house attraction, complete with actors jumping out at visitors.
  • She was shown clips from some of the most fear-inducing films ever made — The Blair Witch Project, The Shining, and The Silence of the Lambs.

Across all three tests, S.M. showed no fear whatsoever. Not suppressed fear, not bravery in the face of fear — genuine absence of it. Instead, she reported interest, curiosity, and excitement. She reached out to touch the tarantula. She wanted to know more about the "monsters" jumping out at her in the haunted house.

But here's the detail researchers found just as interesting: when she was shown film clips that weren't about fear — ones designed to trigger happiness, or disgust — she reacted completely normally, with appropriate emotional expressions matching the content.

PART THREE

A Life Without Fear

Outside the lab, S.M.'s fearlessness shapes almost everything about how she moves through the world. She's described as extremely outgoing, warm, and uninhibited — she has an unusually strong pull toward approaching and physically engaging with other people, even complete strangers.

She also has almost no sense of personal space. She's comfortable standing nose-to-nose with a stranger, maintaining direct eye contact, in a way most people would find intensely uncomfortable. Interestingly, she does understand the concept of personal space intellectually — she just doesn't feel the discomfort that would normally regulate it for herself.

Researchers also found she has real trouble with something specific: reading negative social cues. She's markedly impaired at recognizing fear in other people's facial expressions, and struggles to judge how trustworthy or approachable a stranger's face is, even when the face is showing clear warning signs.

This combination — indiscriminate approach, no personal-space discomfort, and difficulty reading danger in faces — has had serious real-world consequences. Over her lifetime, S.M. has been held up at both knifepoint and gunpoint, was nearly killed in a domestic violence incident, and has received explicit death threats on multiple occasions.

In each case, witnesses and researchers noted the same thing: she showed none of the urgency, desperation, or self-protective behavior that would normally accompany a life-threatening situation.

PART FOUR

The Twist

For years, S.M.'s case seemed to tell a clean story: the amygdala is the brain's "fear center," and without it, fear simply doesn't happen. Then, in a later study, researchers tried one more thing.

Instead of showing her something frightening to look at, they had her breathe in a mixture of air with a higher-than-normal concentration of carbon dioxide — a safe, temporary manipulation that produces the physical sensation of suffocating, even though the person is in no real danger.

S.M. — and other patients with the same rare bilateral amygdala damage — didn't just react. They experienced fear and panic attacks that were more intense than those of neurologically healthy people breathing the exact same mixture.

S.M. described the feeling as completely novel to her — something she had never experienced before, not in the haunted house, not with the tarantula, not watching The Shining.

Researchers also found something else notable about her memory. Normally, emotionally intense experiences get "tagged" for stronger long-term memory storage — a process that depends on the amygdala. In S.M., this enhancement doesn't happen: she remembers emotional and non-emotional information at the same, ordinary strength. Her memory for neutral information works completely normally.

Thinking Questions

  1. This seems to contradict everything from Parts Two and Three. How could someone with no amygdala feel intense panic from suffocation, but zero fear from a tarantula or a horror movie?
  2. What might this suggest about whether "fear" is really just one single system in the brain?

PART FIVE

The Scientific Interpretation

S.M.'s case ended up doing more than confirming the amygdala matters for fear — it helped researchers realize fear itself isn't one single system.

  • The amygdala appears essential for processing external, learned threat cues — things you perceive with your senses and have to recognize as dangerous: a spider, a threatening face, a horror movie, a knife pulled on you in the street. This matches decades of research on amygdala-dependent fear conditioning, where the amygdala links a cue (like a tone or an image) to a threat response.
  • Her impaired ability to read fear in other people's faces, and to judge trustworthiness, fits this same system: recognizing danger signals from the outside world is exactly what her damaged structure would normally support.
  • Her lack of personal-space discomfort and indiscriminate social approach reflect the same gap — normally, the amygdala helps generate the felt sense of "this is too close, this is risky," which shapes behavior even when you consciously know the social rule.
  • Her memory differences fit too: the amygdala normally helps tag emotionally arousing experiences for stronger memory consolidation. Without it, emotional and neutral experiences get remembered at the same ordinary strength.
  • But the suffocation finding pointed to something the amygdala doesn't control: interoceptive threat — danger signals that come from inside the body itself, like rising CO2 levels signaling suffocation. That kind of panic appears to run through a different, older pathway, involving structures like the brainstem, that doesn't require the amygdala at all — and may even normally be partly regulated by the amygdala, which could explain why S.M. and other amygdala-damaged patients panicked more intensely, not less.

The current scientific interpretation, in short: the amygdala isn't a single "fear center" for all fear everywhere. It's specifically critical for detecting and learning from external threat cues in the environment — while a separate, more primitive system handles internal bodily threat signals like suffocation, and that system may not need the amygdala's involvement at all.

Thinking Questions

  1. Which part of this interpretation do you find most convincing? Which part feels like it needs more evidence?
  2. Does splitting "fear" into an external-threat system and an internal-threat system change how you'd define fear as a psychological concept?

Your Task

Is the amygdala really the brain's "fear center" — or does S.M.'s case suggest something more complicated?

Consider these angles as you write:

Two-Systems View

LeDoux's model of amygdala-dependent "learned" fear vs. Panksepp's separate brainstem-based panic/suffocation system. Does treating "fear" as two different systems, rather than one, better explain S.M.'s pattern of results?

Learning History

Could some of S.M.'s behavior reflect a learning deficit rather than a feeling deficit? If she can't detect threat cues in the first place, she'd never get the chance to learn caution around them — regardless of whether she's capable of "feeling" fear internally.

Modularity of Emotion

Does this case support the idea that emotions are built from separate, specialized brain modules rather than one unified "emotion center"? What would that mean for other emotions like disgust or happiness?

Real-World Implications

Given her repeated history of victimization, should S.M.'s case change how we think about the adaptive function of fear — not as an unpleasant feeling to avoid, but as a system that actively keeps people safe?
