Psych Simulations · Core Study Breakdown

Gene x Environment: Thompson et al. (2011)

What's oxytocin got to do with it?

Oxytocin is a hormone that plays a big role in bonding, trust, and how we respond to social and emotional stress. It works by attaching to receptors on cells — think of the receptor as a lock, and oxytocin as the key. The gene that builds that receptor is called OXTR.

Some people carry a variation of the OXTR gene that changes how well that receptor works. On its own, researchers have found this variation doesn't seem to cause problems. It just means a person's stress-response system might be a little more sensitive under the right — or wrong — conditions.

What does "gene x environment interaction" mean?

Most of the time, we think of genes and environment as two separate things that each push your development in their own direction. A "gene x environment interaction" is different: it's when a gene only matters because of a specific environment, or an environment only matters because of a specific gene. Neither one does much alone — the effect only shows up when both are present together, like two ingredients that are harmless separately but react when combined.

Why this matters for this study Growing up with a mother who has struggled with depression is a known environmental stressor for children. Thompson and colleagues wanted to know: does having the OXTR variation make a girl more vulnerable to that specific stressor — even if her mother isn't currently depressed?

How the study worked

The researchers studied 92 adolescent girls, ages 9 to 14. Each girl's mother was interviewed about her mental health history, and each girl's DNA was collected (from a saliva sample) to check for the OXTR variation. Girls also completed a standard anxiety measure, and clinical interviews were carried out with both mothers and daughters.

Mothers were sorted into two groups based on their history:

Low adversity (about 65% of mothers) — no history of Major Depressive Disorder or any other mental illness.

High adversity (about 35% of mothers) — a history of recurrent Major Depressive Disorder (at least two depressive episodes during the daughter's lifetime), but currently in remission — meaning these mothers were not depressed at the time of the study.

Crossing that with whether each girl carried the OXTR variation or not creates four possible groups — which is exactly what you're about to work through.

The key insight

Only one of the four groups showed elevated symptoms: girls with the OXTR variation and a mother with a history of recurrent depression. Having the gene variation alone didn't matter. Having a mother with a depression history alone didn't matter. It took both together — which is exactly what "gene x environment interaction" means: a genetic vulnerability that only shows its effect when a specific environmental stressor is also present.
