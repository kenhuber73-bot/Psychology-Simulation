Membrane potential at the axon initial segment (mV vs. time)

1. Resting Potential — about −70 mV
  • At rest, the membrane is much more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺ because there are many K⁺ leak channels.
  • K⁺ tends to leave the neuron, leaving the inside relatively negative.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains the concentration gradients by moving 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in.
  • The pump contributes slightly to the voltage, but the difference in ion permeability, especially K⁺ leak, is the main reason for the resting potential.
2. Stimulus → Graded Potential
  • A stimulus opens ion channels and changes the membrane potential.
  • A weak stimulus produces a small, local depolarization.
  • This is a graded potential: stronger stimulus → larger change.
  • It spreads passively but gets weaker with distance.
3. Threshold — about −55 mV
  • If the depolarization reaches threshold, usually at the axon initial segment, enough voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open to trigger an action potential.
  • If threshold is not reached, there is no action potential.
  • This is where the all-or-none principle begins.
4. Depolarization
  • Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open rapidly.
  • Na⁺ rushes into the neuron.
  • The inside becomes less negative and then temporarily positive.
  • This rapidly drives the membrane potential upward.
5. Repolarization
  • Na⁺ channels quickly become inactivated.
  • Voltage-gated K⁺ channels, which opened more slowly, are now strongly open.
  • K⁺ flows out of the neuron.
  • The membrane potential becomes negative again.
6. Hyperpolarization
  • K⁺ channels close relatively slowly.
  • K⁺ continues leaving even after the membrane has returned toward −70 mV.
  • The membrane therefore becomes more negative than resting potential.
  • Eventually the K⁺ channels close and the membrane returns toward resting potential.
7. Refractory Period
  • The membrane cannot immediately fire another normal action potential.
  • During the absolute refractory period, another action potential is impossible because Na⁺ channels are inactivated.
  • During the relative refractory period, another action potential is possible but requires a stronger stimulus.
  • This creates an important asymmetry: the region behind the action potential is refractory, while the region ahead is ready to respond.
8. How the Signal Moves
  • Na⁺ entering one region makes the inside of that region temporarily more positive.
  • This creates local electrical currents that spread in both directions along the axon.
  • The current spreads into the region ahead and depolarizes it.
  • If that region reaches threshold, its voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open.
  • A new action potential is generated in the next region.
  • Meanwhile, the region behind is refractory and cannot immediately fire again.
  • Therefore, the signal effectively moves forward.

The key idea: the action potential does not physically travel down the axon as one continuous electrical wave. Instead, each section of the axon triggers the next section to generate a new action potential.

9. Myelin → Saltatory Conduction
  • Myelin electrically insulates the axon.
  • Local current can therefore spread rapidly underneath the myelin.
  • Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are concentrated at the nodes of Ranvier.
  • The action potential is regenerated mainly at these nodes.
  • The signal therefore appears to "jump" from node to node.
  • This is called saltatory conduction.
10. Why Does It Only Go One Way?
  • The electrical current itself spreads both forward and backward.
  • But the membrane behind the action potential is in its refractory period.
  • The membrane ahead is still capable of reaching threshold.

Therefore: Na⁺ enters → local current spreads → next region reaches threshold → new action potential → previous region is refractory → forward propagation.

11. Equilibrium Potentials
  • Na⁺ equilibrium potential ≈ +60 mV
  • K⁺ equilibrium potential ≈ −90 to −93 mV
  • An ion's equilibrium potential is the voltage at which its electrical force and concentration gradient balance each other.
  • The membrane does not actually reach these values during a normal action potential because Na⁺ channels inactivate and K⁺ channels eventually close.
